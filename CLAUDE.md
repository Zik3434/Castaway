# Castaway

A pixel-art fishing and hunting game. You are a fisherman stranded on an island. You fish your way off it, then fish and hunt your way across a fictional sea of wildly different islands, buying rods, lines, bait, weapons and boats, and (around 200 hours in) a starship, to fish in space. Travel is by chart; on each island you walk a short strip between the fishing spot, the village and the hunting ground.

`index.html` is the entire playable prototype (vanilla JS, canvas, no build step). Open it in a browser. Read this file before changing anything; it holds every design decision made so far, what is placeholder, and what comes next.

## Decisions already made (do not relitigate without asking)

- **Stranded is the hook, and it is used.** You start on an island with no market. Fish go in a bucket. Driftwood comes up on the lines; six pieces build a raft. The raft is the first boat. The first port buys the whole haul, and that is the moment money starts to exist. Selling is irrelevant until you leave the island.
- **Passive lines bring volume and cash. Skill lines bring trophies.** A set line hooks common fish with no fight and never reaches trophy size. The rod gives a bite you must answer within a few seconds, then a bar-and-fish fight; rare species come only from the rod or the trophy line.
- **The trophy line is the expensive passive line.** Long wait, needs better bait, hooks big fish, but you still have to land it in the fight when you come back. It exists so patience pays without replacing skill.
- **"Leave the rod out and wait" means during a session, while you work another line.** Passive lines also keep ticking while the game is closed: a hooked fish is saved as hooked and waits for you. Passive lines do not earn money offline on their own; you still have to land and sell.
- **The world is a branching chart of very different islands, revealed by neighbours and rumors.** Boat tiers: raft 1, skiff 2, cutter 3, trawler 4, clipper 5, ironclad 6, starship 7 (space). Every tier has two islands and routes cross between them, so there are always several paths. An island is hidden until it is linked to somewhere you have been (then a fogged `???` you can still sail to if your boat reaches) or until a shopkeeper names it in a rumor (then its name and rumor line show, marked with a star). Shopkeepers from tier 3 on (Old Sajan at the delta, and everyone beyond) hear about the exotic islands first. Rod → bigger fish → money → boat → range → new islands → rarer fish is still the ladder.
- **Every island is its own culture, One Piece style, and they get more exotic with distance.** Tiers 1–4 are real places with real fish and animals: a salt-traders' cay, a lantern-lit river wharf, a monsoon delta on stilts, a drowned city's reef, a frozen fjord. Tier 5 on is exotic with invented fish and game: a volcano island whose fish swim in fire, an atoll of water like air, a lake inside a mountain, shoals under a storm that never moves. Space is tier 7. The current set is in `LOCS`; the bank of ideas for more is below.
- **Each island is a strip of three screens you walk:** the hunting gate on the left, the village in the middle, the fishing shore on the right (the normal fishing scene). Each has one thing to press: the gate into the wild, the village building (the shop, the traders' tent, or your camp), and the fishing spot. The shop is no longer a tab; you walk to it. Walking is the only character movement in the game and it is deliberately short.
- **Every island has 3–5 named fishing spots, each known for something.** `spots` on an island: a name, a "known for" line, and a roster of species multipliers. Rosters overlap (the commons appear at several spots) but every spot has fish the others lack, and each legend lives at exactly one spot. The pool is spot-based: weight = the species' island weight × the spot multiplier, so a spot with no entry for a fish never gives it. Spots are picked from a sheet (the Spots tab on the fishing screen, or the fishing spot on the island strip); moving pulls in your lines. The current spot shows in the HUD clock line.
- **The chart is a map of the whole world, blacked out.** One sea with every island as a drawn landmass and a few uninhabited sandbars, under near-black fog that clears in a wide circle around islands you have visited, a smaller one around islands a keeper named, a sliver around neighbouring land you have only heard of (the `???`), and along routes you have sailed between visited islands. Space (The Stars) is a glyph, not land. The fog is an ImageData layer cached by visited/known sets.
- **Hunting is the second loop.** Behind every island's gate is a hunting ground with its own creatures (real animals on real islands, invented ones beyond). A stalk is wait → sighting → draw → loose: a mark swings along a bar, you hold to draw (the mark slows, your breath runs out), and let go with the mark inside the band to take the animal. Band width is the weapon (sling, bow, crossbow, bought in shops) less the animal's skittishness; swing speed is the animal; wait too long and it bolts. Game goes in a bag (cap 10) and sells with the fish. Creatures have their own index.
- **Own art, higher resolution than Stardew.** The bar-and-fish fight mechanic is fair game (mechanics are not protected); the art, sounds, names and UI are ours. Nothing is copied from Stardew Valley or One Cast. Everything in the prototype is placeholder code-drawn pixel art at 192×108 scene resolution; the resolution decision for real art is still open (see below).
- **Space is the endgame and the pitch line, not a reskin.** Space fishing only earns its place if the mechanics change (gravity throws off the net, alien behaviors). Build hours 1–20 first; space is an expansion.
- **The world is fictional, the fish are real.** Island names are placeholders until the world is named. Every species outside space is a real fish, placed by biome (warm shallows, temperate pier, kelp forest, deep sea, cold fjord), with its real adult size range. Space gets invented species later.
- **One legend per location.** Each island has an invented, named legendary fish (Wreckwraith, The Harbormaster, The Saltwidow, Verdigris, The Jade Lantern, Nightmaw, Monsoon Mother, Rimeheart, The Bellringer, Caldera, The Unseen, The Deep Choir, Galvanis) with a line of lore. Rod only, squid, a narrow time window, a sliver of pool weight (0.4 against commons at 20–40), difficulty 0.97+, and it can be landed once ever: once it is in the index it leaves the pool. Losing the fight leaves it out there.
- **Worth is deliberately unrealistic.** A 4 lb mullet is about $55. Worth comes from weight: `rate × kg^0.75`, ±15% rolled at catch time, ×1.5 at trophy size, floor $3. `rate` is per species and is not proportional to size or rarity on purpose (deep-sea oddities and a garibaldi are worth far more per kilo than a cod), so the curve bends.

## Open questions and placeholders

- World name and island names: placeholders. Current set by tier: 0 Wreck Shoal (start) · 1 Gull Harbor (shop), Brine Cay (sells) · 2 Kelp Reach, Lantern Wharf (shop) · 3 Black Trench (buyer boat), Monsoon Delta (shop, rumors) · 4 Frost Fjords (shop), Bell Reef (sells) · 5 Cinder Isle (shop), Glasswater Atoll (shop) · 6 Hollow Mountain (shop), Thunderhead Shoals (shop) · 7 The Stars (locked). 111 fish including 13 legends, 37 creatures.
- Island idea bank, so we never run out (add from here as the chart grows; each needs a scene, a village building, a hunting ground, a roster, a keeper and a legend): a floating market of rafts lashed together that drifts between tiers; a graveyard of ships where the "island" is hulls; a monastery on a sea stack with monks who fish by bell; a pirate cove that buys stolen fish at double and sells nothing legal; a whaling town gone quiet, bones for houses; a coral city of divers who hunt with spears underwater; a sargasso sea you row through, fish in the weed; a tidal island reachable only at low tide, with a clock; a crater lake of hot springs and blind white crayfish; an island of giant tortoises and a lighthouse keeper who talks to them; a salt-crusted dead sea where nothing sinks and fish swim upside down; an ice-locked ship frozen in a berg, the crew still trading; a mangrove maze with a witch who sells bait that always bites once; a bird island white with guano, eggs instead of fish; a steppe island of horse people who fish rivers with eagles; a bioluminescent bay where fishing is only at night; a sunken-volcano lagoon with a hole to the deep; a kingdom of frogs on lily pads the size of boats; an upside-down island hanging from a cloud (tier 6–7); a glass desert where the fish are mirages until hooked; a clockwork island of automaton fishermen; a graveyard of leviathans, fishing from a ribcage; a storm-eye island calm in the middle of a permanent hurricane; an island that is itself a sleeping fish; the first space island: a tide pool on the moon.
- Art resolution. Prototype scene is 192×108 logical pixels. "Higher quality than Stardew" means more pixels per sprite; every step up multiplies asset cost. Decide before drawing anything real. Suggestion: 320×180 scene, 24–32 px character.
- Real-time vs accelerated clock. Prototype: 1 real minute = 1 game hour (24-minute day) so bite windows are testable. Passive-line timers are real time (`Date.now()`), so they survive closing the game.
- All numbers below are prototype values at roughly one-tenth of intended wait times. Prices and fish values are untuned guesses.
- Travel cost. Sailing is free in the prototype (it pulls in every line). Fuel or tickets per trip are an option.
- Bucket cap (20) and what "bucket full" means at a location with no market. Prototype: the catch is recorded in the index and released.
- What makes hour 50 feel different from hour 5. Current answer: the fish index (count, best size, trophy count per species). Likely additions: rare variants, weather, size records with names.

## Presentation decisions (second pass)

- **The scene is the whole screen.** One fixed canvas fills the viewport; the HUD, the tide line and the control panel float over it. No screen-in-a-screen. The panel is translucent so the water shows through.
- **Logical resolution adapts to the device.** `layout()` picks an integer scale (phone: 2 → about 195×375 logical px; desktop: 3–4) so pixels stay crisp. The surface line sits at 40% of the height in portrait, 52% in landscape. Everything is positioned relative to `W`, `H`, `SURF` and `shoreW`; never hardcode pixel positions.
- **Own look, not One Cast's.** Dithered sky and sea gradients (2×2 Bayer, cached per hour), a big seated fisherman (24×30) with a wide hat and beard, a sun that rises over the water on the right and sets behind the island on the left, light shafts, a sandy seabed that darkens with depth, per-location foregrounds (beach with wreck and palm, pier with shack and lantern, rocks with kelp, your own boat at the trench, an ice shelf with aurora and snow). The map is a parchment chart with ink islands, dotted routes and a compass, not a world map.
- **The title screen is a screensaver.** `titleBeat` runs a timed cycle (wait of varying length → bite → reel in → bait up → cast → settle): the float bobs and ripples, dips on the bite, the rod bends and the float comes in to the tip, the rod goes upright while a bait pixel appears on the hook, the float arcs back out and splashes. Every other cycle a small fish jumps right of the boat. Everything is a function of time, so it costs nothing and never desyncs.
- **New voyage starts with a title card, then a playable prologue, then the storm.** A card first (`MODE === 'intro'`): the empty sea at dusk, a skiff far off, the line *Somewhere in the ocean…*, about four seconds or a tap. Then the player begins in the skiff on a calm open sea (hidden location `sea`, never on the chart, its own deep seabed) with the rod only: no map, no shop, no money, one line card. **The prologue is the tutorial**: `tutorialText` turns the tide line into a brass instruction box that answers "what does the game want right now" from state (cast → watch the float → reel on the hit → hold to lift the net → keep or release → one more), the Cast button pulses when it is the player's move, and `rollCatch` forces the first fish to be an easy one (difficulty ≤ 0.2) so the first fight teaches. The real cast/bite/fight loop runs. When the second fish is kept or released, "The light is going." and the cutscene starts. Four beats, each tappable, skippable, captions kept to a few words and left unexplained: *No warning.* (storm) → *He did not let go.* (drift) → *Morning. Somewhere.* (washed up) → *Nobody is coming.* (sits up). When it ends the save resets to the shoal with no boat, empty bucket, rod and set line; the index keeps the two prologue fish, so the story is in the record. Closing the game mid-storm resumes the storm. Continue skips all of it. The menu replays the cutscene only.
- **Captions crossfade** (`setCap` swaps the text only after the fade-out) and do not use `text-wrap: balance`, which drew ghost glyphs on iOS Safari.
- **Wipe protection.** Start over asks for a second tap.

## Catch screen and control panel (third pass)

- **Every landing opens a catch screen.** Headline by priority (Trophy! → New record! → New species! → Landed!), tag pills for the rest, the fish lit in a dark panel, name, size, a sub line (rig, time since the line went out), and three stat tiles: worth, bait, time of day. Driftwood and junk get the same screen with a single button. The size line shows length and weight; weight comes from `kgOf` (a condition factor per body shape in `K_SHAPE`, overridable per species with `k`, times length cubed, with ±8% jitter rolled at catch time and stored as `kg`). The index keeps `bestKg` beside `best`; the inventory lists both.
- **The fish is held until you decide.** At a market: **Sell** (that one fish, money now) or **Keep** (bucket, sell the haul later). Elsewhere: **Keep** or **Release**. Bucket full: Keep is disabled and Release is offered. Tapping outside the card keeps the fish (or releases it if the bucket is full). The pending catch lives in `S.pending` and is saved, so a reload reopens the screen. The index is updated at landing time, so a released fish still counts as caught.
- **The panel is the always-there tab.** Tide line; `LINES` header with a live "n lines ready" / "n on!" count; four line cards; a compact `ROD · SCRAPS  CHANGE` row where tapping the rig or bait chip cycles to the next option and CHANGE unfolds the full rig and bait pickers; the rig note; the big Cast / Set the line / Pull in / Reel in / Haul in button; then a bottom bar with **Inventory** (bucket count and value) and, at a market, **Sell haul**. The driftwood row stays above the bar on the shoal before the raft.
- **Inventory sheet.** Bucket list with sprites, size, trophy star, value and a Let go button per fish; whole-bucket total and Sell the haul at a market; bait counts; gear (rod, boat, lines, trophy rig, driftwood).
- **Every species has its own sprite, built from a body plan.** `FISH_ART` gives each species a plan (`gen`: size, body depth, where the body is deepest, peduncle and snout thickness, tail type `fork`/`lunate`/`hetero`/`round`/`trunc`/`none`, dorsal and anal fins by type and span, adipose fin, pelvic fin, barbels, a beak, an underbite, open `jaws` with teeth, an angler's lure, a tail light) or a hand-drawn silhouette (`sil`, used for the sunfish), plus a palette (`col`: a saturated body colour, a bright belly, fin, mark, glow) and a pattern (`pat`: bars, saddles, stripes, lateral line, spots, scales, lights, bands, scutes…). `genSil` rasterises the silhouette on a padded canvas and trims it; `shadeSil` adds the outline, sheen, back/mid/belly tones, fin rays, the eye and the pattern. `fishPal` derives the shades the way hand-pixelled fish are coloured: shadows darker and shifted toward blue, the highlight lighter and warmer, the outline a deep tint of the body rather than black. Composition follows the pixel-fish idiom without copying any sprite: chunky proportions (bodies compressed toward about 2:1, deep-bodied fish nearly round), thick fins and tails, a big white eye (3×3 on deep bodies) with a glint on the head, and five tones of volume from back to belly: a bright rim under the outline, a dark band, body colour, a lit lower band, then the belly. Patterns stay off the rim so the volume reads. That five-step gradient is what makes the sprites look rounded rather than flat. Sprites run 24–54 px wide and 11–28 tall, two to four times the pixels of a 16 px fish, in line with the "higher resolution than Stardew" decision. They appear on the catch screen (always 3×, so size differences show), in the index (unknown species as a dark silhouette), in the inventory and in the fight. The swimming fish in the scene stay one-colour silhouettes on purpose: they are far off and under murky water. The fisherman and the scenes have not had their real art pass yet.

`MODE` is `title` / `intro` (the card before the prologue) / `cut` / `game` (the fishing shore) / `island` (the strip) / `hunt`. Sailing lands you on the fishing shore. Passive lines keep resolving in every mode but title and cut.

Line state machine: `idle → waiting → bite (rod, 3 s window) → fight → idle` or `idle → waiting → hooked (set/trophy, waits forever) → haul or fight → idle`. One rod can be active at a time; set lines can all be out at once.

Catch value: `valueOf` = `rate × kg^0.75 × (1 ± 0.15)`, ×1.5 at trophy size (sizeFraction ≥ 0.85), floor $3. Weight comes from `kgOf`. Trophy line skews size up (`u^0.45`), set line skews down and caps at 0.8, rod is linear.

## How to run and test

- Open `index.html` in any browser. Fonts load from Google Fonts; everything else is inline. Works offline except the fonts (monospace fallback).
- Reset a save: Menu → Back to title → Start over (tap twice), or in the browser console `localStorage.removeItem('castaway-proto-v2')` and reload.
- Visual check without a phone: Playwright with a 390×760 mobile viewport, screenshot the title, each cutscene beat (click `#cut` to advance) and the game. Animated buttons (`.cta.hit`) need `{ force: true }` to click in Playwright.
- To test the island loop fast, lower `T.setWait` or raise the `wood` weight in `SPECIES`.
- To check the catch tables without a browser: extract the script and run `pool()` for every location × phase × bait × rig; none should be empty (last run, with the real roster: 160 pools, zero empty, ~28% driftwood on the shoal day rod).

## Art direction

- Side-on cross-section filling the screen: sky, surface line, underwater, with the shore on the left third and open water to the right. The fisherman sits at the water's edge on something that belongs to the location (wreck plank, pier end, rock, his own deck, ice). Lines go out to floats spread across the water.
- Palette: tar navy ground, brass accents, lantern light. Day cycle through dawn/day/dusk/night sky keyframes; the sun rises over the sea and sets behind the island.
- Each island needs its own shore, seabed, village building, hunting ground, fish roster, creatures, keeper lines and legend. The data-driven `scene` + `village` + `hunt` descriptors and the prop and building tables exist so that a new island is mostly data; the five oldest shores are still hand-drawn. Budget content, not code: an island is only worth adding when it feels nothing like the one before.
- Replace the string-mask sprites with real sprite sheets once the resolution is decided. Keep `image-rendering: pixelated` and integer scaling. Keep the caching pattern: anything static per hour or per location is rendered once to an offscreen canvas.

## Roadmap, in order

1. Tune the first 20 minutes: time to raft, first sale amount, time to skiff. Target: raft in ~4 minutes, harbor in 5, a reason to come back to the shoal (Ghost Tarpon at night).
2. Fight feel: per-species behaviors are table stakes. Add one twist that makes the mechanic ours (line tension that snaps if you hold too long, or a second axis).
3. Index as the long-term driver: size records, rare variants, catch log per location.
4. Weather and seasons as bite gates.
5. Audio: a bite thump, a reel, a sale chime. Original.
6. Real art pass at the chosen resolution.
7. Split the single file into modules. It passed 2,000 lines with the world expansion (now ~2,500): data (islands, species, creatures), art (generator, props, buildings), scenes, modes. Do this before the next big content pass.
8. Space: new physics in the fight, alien roster, the Starship at $500,000 stays as the visible endgame price.

## Conventions for working in this repo

- Keep it dependency-free and single-file until the roadmap says otherwise. No frameworks, no bundler.
- Every wait, price and weight is data in the tables at the top; change numbers there, not in logic.
- Never add art, names, sounds or UI copied from existing games.
- When adding an island: an entry in `LOCS` with links both ways, a `scene` (or a hand-drawn shore), a `village`, a `hunt`, 3–5 `spots` each with a known-for line and a roster (every island fish in at least one spot, commons in several, the legend in one), a keeper with three lines and a rumor, 5–7 fish with sprites (real for tiers 1–4, invented from tier 5), 2–4 creatures (a silhouette from `BEAST_SIL` and a palette), and a legend with lore. Then the pool check: every spot × phase × bait × rig non-empty, and every hunting ground at every phase.
- When adding a species: pick a real fish that belongs in that location's biome (invented only on exotic islands); add location weight, time window, real size range, value, behavior, difficulty, min bait, rare flag, scene `shape`, and a `FISH_ART` entry (a body plan and palette; copy the closest existing plan and adjust, or a hand-drawn `sil` for odd shapes). Then re-run the empty-pool check. Judge sprites on a contact sheet first: render every `fishMap` at 3× in a scratch page.
- Fish are drawn head right, tail left; the scene flips them as needed.
- Save format is versioned (`v: 8`). Bump it and write a migration in `load()` when the shape changes.
- Testing in Playwright: the game saves on `visibilitychange`, so a reload overwrites anything you put in `localStorage` from the page. Seed a save with `context.addInitScript` in a fresh context instead.
- Reference material the designer liked: One Cast (onecast.github.io) for the fast-travel, multi-line, location-and-bite-window structure; Stardew Valley for the fight mechanic. Reference only.
